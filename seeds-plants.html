Seeds & Plants Guide

Choose the right plants for your space, sow them at the right time, and keep them healthy with the right amount of light and water. Everything here is aimed at gardeners growing across the United States.

Start here

Pick the right plant for your space

Before you buy anything, consider three things: the light your space gets, the time you can spend, and your local climate. A plant that matches your conditions and your schedule will be far easier to keep healthy than one that does not.

  • Light: How much direct sun does your spot get each day?
  • Time: Are you able to water most days, or only occasionally?
  • Climate: What is your USDA hardiness zone?
See watering needs
Know your climate

US hardiness zones, simply explained

The USDA hardiness zone map tells you the lowest winter temperature your area typically reaches. Knowing your zone helps you choose plants that can survive your winters and plan when to sow seeds.

Zones 2–4

Very cold winters. Short growing season — start seeds indoors and harden them off before the last frost.

Zones 5–6

Cold to moderate winters and a clear spring. A wide range of vegetables, flowers, and perennials thrive here.

Zones 7–8

Milder winters and longer seasons. Many warm-season crops can be planted earlier and grow later.

Zones 9–11

Mild to tropical winters. Year-round growing is possible for many crops; focus on heat-loving plants in summer.

Find your exact zone

You can find your precise zone by entering your ZIP code into the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map. Your local cooperative extension office can also tell you your region's average last frost date — the key date for outdoor sowing.

How to sow seeds

From packet to seedling in six steps

Most seeds need warmth, moisture, and light to germinate. These basics cover the majority of common vegetable, herb, and flower seeds.

Read the seed packet

Check the recommended sowing depth, spacing, and whether the seed wants to be started indoors or sown directly outside. Seed packets list days to maturity and ideal conditions.

Use fresh, well-draining soil

Choose a seed-starting mix or potting soil. It should hold moisture but drain well, and be free of large clumps.

Sow at the right depth

As a rule of thumb, plant seeds about two to three times their diameter deep. Fine seeds are often just pressed onto the surface and lightly firmed.

Keep evenly moist

Water gently so you do not wash seeds away. A spray bottle or a fine rose on a watering can works well for small trays.

Provide warmth and light

Most seeds germinate best between 65–75°F (18–24°C). Once sprouts appear, move them to bright light so they do not become tall and leggy.

Harden off before planting out

Before setting seedlings outdoors, gradually introduce them to sun and wind over 5–7 days. This reduces transplant shock.

Ornamental & outdoor

Flowers, shrubs, and perennials

Ornamental plants are grown for colour, texture, and structure. Many are perennials that come back year after year, while annuals bloom for a single season and are replanted each spring.

  • Annuals (like marigolds, petunias, zinnias): bloom for one season and are replanted annually.
  • Perennials (like coneflowers, daylilies, lavender): return each year and generally need less replanting.
  • Shrubs & climbers (like roses, clematis, hydrangeas): add height, structure, and long-lasting interest.

Check the plant's tag or seed packet for its mature spread and sunlight needs before planting. This helps you space plants correctly from the start.

Indoor plants

Bring the garden inside

Houseplants are a wonderful way to keep gardening going year-round. Most indoor plants succeed when you match them to the light you actually have.

  • Low light: snake plants, pothos, and ZZ plants tolerate dim corners and irregular watering.
  • Bright, indirect light: spider plants, peace lilies, and philodendrons thrive near a sunny window.
  • Direct sun: succulents and most cacti like the brightest windowsill you have.

For houseplants, use pots with drainage holes and a well-draining mix. Overwatering is the most common beginner mistake.

A simple watering rule

Stick a finger about an inch into the soil. If it feels dry, water; if it still feels moist, wait. Watering less often but more deeply usually encourages stronger roots than frequent, shallow sprinkles.
