Backyard & Rooftop Farming

Grow your own vegetables and fruit in the space you have — a backyard, a raised bed, a container, or a rooftop. These practical steps help you plan a productive season without guesswork.

Where to grow

Find the best spot in your space

Most edible crops want at least six hours of direct sunlight a day, good drainage, and protection from strong wind. That makes a sunny patch of ground, a raised bed, or a bright balcony a great starting point.

  • In the ground: easiest for root crops and larger plants with room to spread.
  • Raised beds: better drainage, warmer soil early in the season, and easier to weed.
  • Containers: perfect for small spaces, herbs, lettuce, peppers, and more.
  • Rooftop / balcony: grows in containers — just watch water, weight, and wind.
Rooftop & small-space tips

Growing upward and in pots

Urban and rooftop gardens have a few extra considerations, but they can be very productive. A little planning goes a long way.

  • Weight: Use lightweight soil blends and avoid overloading a single area.
  • Drainage: Every container needs holes; consider saucers to protect surfaces.
  • Wind: Choose sturdy pots and add stakes for tall crops like tomatoes.
  • Water: Container soil dries faster — check it often in warm weather.
What to grow first

Easy vegetables, tasty fruits

These crops are dependable for beginners and rewarding to harvest. Start with a few you already enjoy eating.

Beginner vegetables

  • Lettuce & salad greens — quick, cool-weather, and easy in containers.
  • Tomatoes — need sun and support; great in a raised bed or large pot.
  • Zucchini & squash — productive, but give them room to spread.
  • Radishes — ready in as little as a few weeks, so great for kids.
  • Bush beans — simple, quick, and do not need a trellis.
  • Peppers — love warm weather and even moisture.

Fruits to try

  • Strawberries — grow well in beds, containers, or hanging baskets.
  • Tomatoes — technically a fruit; a reliable backyard favorite.
  • Blueberries — need acidic soil and a few bushes for best pollination.
  • Dwarf fruit trees — apples, peaches, and figs come in compact varieties.
  • Melons & cucumbers — warm-season climbers that like strong sun.
  • Squash — some types are grown for their flowers and young fruit.

Check local fruit rules

Some states and regions place rules on certain fruit trees — for example, restrictions on citrus in parts of the country. Before planting a fruit tree, check with your state agriculture department or local extension office.

Healthy soil, happy plants

Soil and companion planting basics

Good soil and smart plant pairings make a big difference. A little preparation pays off all season.

Improve your soil

Add compost or well-rotted organic matter before planting. It improves drainage, holds moisture, and feeds soil life. Mix it into the top few inches of your bed.

Mulch to protect

A 2–3 inch layer of straw or shredded leaves around plants keeps moisture in, keeps soil cool, and helps suppress weeds.

Rotate your crops

Avoid planting the same crop family in the same spot each year. This helps reduce soil-borne pests and keeps nutrients balanced.

Plant basil near tomatoes

Many gardeners pair basil with tomatoes. The basil does not need much space, and both enjoy similar sun and water.

Marigolds beside squash

Marigolds are a popular, bright-flowered companion plant often used around squash and other vegetables.

Carrots near onions

Some gardeners sow carrots and members of the onion family together. Growing conditions overlap, which makes scheduling easy.
